Fine-tuning moves the model; RL reweights it

Two ways to make a model solve a task more often: fine-tune it on demonstrations, or train it with reinforcement learning on a reward of 1 for a solving answer. Choose how often the trained model should solve the task, and compare how far each method has moved the model to get there.

A companion to Lecture 7, §3: the best policy (13), the odds (15), and Figure 15 of the notes. The comparison follows Shenfeld et al. (2025), RL's Razor: Why Online Reinforcement Learning Forgets Less. An illustration: every number is computed exactly from the setup below.

The setup. The model's answers to one prompt are points on a line. The base model draws them from a standard normal distribution. Answers to the right of 1.2 solve the task; the base model solves it 11.5% of the time.

RL: the best policy of the objective (12) for a reward of 1 for a solving answer, π* ∝ πbase er/β, computed exactly. A smaller β gives a higher success rate.

Fine-tuning: a Gaussian model, starting from the base model, fitted by gradient descent on the log-loss of demonstrations drawn from N(3.3, 0.55²), all of which solve the task. The slider picks the step at which it first solves the task as often as chosen.

What to look for

  1. RL keeps the shape of the base model. Inside the green region, the blue curve is the base model's curve scaled up; outside, scaled down. Every solving answer is multiplied by the same factor, e1/β relative to the others, so the odds of solving are multiplied by e1/β, as in equation (15). No answer appears that the base model did not already write.
  2. Fine-tuning moves the whole distribution toward the demonstrations, including answers that the base model almost never wrote. On its way it first spreads out, then narrows onto the demonstrations.
  3. At the same success rate, fine-tuning has moved further: here at least twice as far, measured as in the penalty of the objective. Shenfeld et al. found the same on real models, and found that this drift predicts how much a model forgets of what it could do before.
  4. No model can solve the task as often with less drift than RL's. A model that solves the task with probability s is at least as far from the base model as the coin that comes up heads with probability s is from the coin with 11.5%, in either order of the divergence. The best policy reaches exactly that bound, because it changes the base model only by one factor inside the region and one outside.
  5. Switch the order of the divergence. The penalty in the objective (12) puts the model first. With the base model first, both drifts grow quickly near 100%, because the base model's answers outside the region become very unlikely under either trained model. Fine-tuning still moves further, by less in the middle of the range, and RL's curve is still the lower bound.
