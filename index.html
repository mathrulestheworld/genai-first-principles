About

This course introduces generative AI through the study of large language models, combining mathematical foundations with hands-on implementation. Over the eight weeks we build a small language model from scratch, small enough to train on a laptop in minutes to an hour: we implement its components, train and fine-tune it, probe its behavior, and evaluate its outputs. The aim is a complete working understanding of the model: how it works, how to build it, and how to investigate what it has learned.

We trace the path from statistical models of language to Transformers, pretraining, post-training, and inference. Throughout, we ask the same questions: what can these models learn and compute, from what data, with what resources, and with what guarantees? The implementation gives a concrete setting in which to explore these questions, connecting theory with experiment.

Lectures

  • Week 1: (Artificial) Intelligence, Learning, Language, and Meaning

    What does it mean for a machine to be intelligent, to learn, or to use language? We begin with a short history, from the first models of computation to today’s language models, and set out what the course will build. After fixing the vocabulary of AI, machine learning, and deep learning, we look at how intelligence, learning, language, and meaning have been approached: Turing’s imitation game, search and learning in game playing, induction and PAC learning, grammars and distributional evidence, and the relationship between form, meaning, and intent. (notes, slides, slides as PowerPoint)

    Hands-on: a tensor-based autograd engine built from scratch, covered during the lecture's hands-on segment: computation graphs, reverse-mode differentiation, and gradient checks. We use it to train logistic regression and a two-layer network—the first piece of the codebase that grows into our language model. (notebook)

  • Week 2: Language Modeling: From Counts to Neural Networks

    We treat text as the output of a random source and build language models by counting: n-gram models, smoothing, and their evaluation with cross-entropy and perplexity. The central part of the lecture is about word embeddings: vectors in which words used alike lie near each other, the idea behind semantic search, vector databases, and recommendation systems. We build them by counting contexts and by prediction (word2vec), and see why the two routes agree. We then turn the counts into a network and build neural language models step by step: a bigram model learned by gradient descent, word vectors, a model that predicts the next word from a fixed window of learned vectors, and recurrent networks that read the whole text so far: backpropagation through time, why their gradients vanish, and the LSTM, which beats the best count models. We end with where recurrent networks were used until Transformers replaced them.

    Hands-on: n-gram models with smoothing, a sampler, and an evaluation harness that measures perplexity; word vectors from co-occurrence counts and from word2vec, trained live and explored in two and three dimensions, with nearest-neighbor search; the bigram model as a network, then factored through word vectors; a neural language model in the style of Bengio et al., trained and examined; character-level RNN and LSTM models to take home: why the plain RNN forgets (saturated units, a memory test), a wider LSTM that beats every n-gram model, samples, temperature, and a cell that tracks quotations; a word-level LSTM. (notebook)

  • Week 3: Attention and Transformers: Computation and Limits

    We start from what recurrent networks could not do and introduce attention and the Transformer architecture. A step-by-step account of the computation explains its time and memory costs and the role of the key–value cache. We then examine Transformers’ computational capabilities and limits, paying attention to the assumptions behind formal results.

    Hands-on: a decoder-only Transformer written from scratch, tested so that later tokens cannot influence earlier predictions, and compared with the Week 2 LSTM on held-out text.

  • Week 4: Pretraining: Data, Optimization, and Scale

    How do tokens, data, and a training objective produce a base language model? We examine tokenization, data preparation, and optimization, then study empirical scaling laws and the allocation of a fixed compute budget. We also introduce the systems techniques that make large-scale training possible.

    Hands-on: a byte-level BPE tokenizer, a data pipeline, and a full training loop. We pretrain our model at three sizes on the same token budget and fit a scaling law to the results.

  • Week 5: From Base Models to Assistants

    We examine what pretrained models can do through completion, prompting, and in-context learning, including ways to elicit reasoning. We then introduce the gap between predicting text and following instructions, and the ideas behind instruction tuning and learning from feedback. This provides the motivation for the training methods developed in Weeks 6 and 7.

    Hands-on: a chat template and supervised fine-tuning, with LoRA written from scratch, that turn our pretrained model into a small chatbot: it writes short stories on request and answers arithmetic questions. A short demonstration of in-context learning.

  • Week 6: Reinforcement Learning for Sequence Generation

    We develop the reinforcement learning foundations needed for language model post-training: bandits, Markov decision processes, value functions, and policy gradients. We then formulate text generation as a sequential decision problem, connecting token choices and response-level rewards to regularized policy optimization.

    Hands-on: value iteration, REINFORCE, and PPO on small decision problems, then REINFORCE applied to our language model.

  • Week 7: Post-Training: Demonstrations, Preferences, and Rewards

    We study how language models are trained using demonstrations, preference judgments, and verifiable rewards. Topics include supervised fine-tuning, reward modeling, reinforcement learning from human feedback, direct preference optimization, and training reasoning models. We examine the objectives behind these methods and failure modes such as reward hacking and sycophancy.

    Hands-on: a reward model, DPO, and GRPO that improve the chatbot, including multi-digit arithmetic with verifiable rewards, while tracking reward, KL divergence from the starting model, and response length.

  • Week 8: Inference: Decoding, Test-Time Compute, Serving, and Evaluation

    We examine how decoding rules shape the distribution of generated text and how additional computation at inference time can improve answers. We then study efficient serving and the tradeoffs among speed, memory, and output quality. Evaluation connects these choices to measured performance, with attention to benchmark contamination and statistical uncertainty.

    Hands-on: temperature, top-k, and nucleus samplers; a key–value cache, with timing measurements; speculative decoding, with a check that its output distribution is unchanged; best-of-n sampling with a verifier.

  • Week 9 (optional): Alignment and Language Model Agents

    We examine how desired model behavior is specified and evaluated, and the limits of these approaches. We then study language models as agents, combining retrieval, tool use, and planning in repeated cycles of observation and action.

    Hands-on: a tool-use loop in which a small model calls a calculator, with checks on the answers it returns.
